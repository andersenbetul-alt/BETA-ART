How to price your first freelance project

The number you are afraid to say out loud is usually the right one. Here is how to get to it without guessing.

Beta Art · 2026-07-15 · 6 min read

Start from the year, not the hour

Decide what you need to earn in a year. Divide by the weeks you will actually work — 44, not 52. Divide by the hours you can actually bill — 20 a week, not 40, because selling, admin and accounting are unpaid. The number that falls out is your floor.

Most people are shocked by it, and then quote below it anyway. The shock is the useful part: it tells you that the hourly rate you had in mind was a salary rate, and a salary comes with holiday pay, sick pay and a pension you now have to fund yourself.

Then stop selling hours

Use the hourly figure to sanity-check a fixed price, never to quote one. Hourly billing punishes you for getting faster and makes the client watch the clock instead of the outcome. Quote the job.

To quote a job: estimate the hours honestly, add 30% for the revisions you have not imagined yet, multiply by your rate, and round up. The 30% is not padding. It is the part of every project that nobody scopes and everybody does.

Say the number and stop talking

The silence after a price is the hardest four seconds in freelancing, and filling it is the most expensive habit in the trade. Most discounts are given to a silence, not to a negotiation.

When they say it is too expensive

Do not reduce the price. Reduce the scope, and say what is coming out. “I can do it for that — it would be the one page rather than the five.” This keeps your rate intact, teaches the client that price tracks scope, and often ends with them finding the original budget.

Raise it earlier than feels comfortable

Raise on new clients first; existing ones keep the old rate for a stated period. If nobody has said no in six months, you are too cheap. A small number of refusals is evidence of correct pricing, not of failure.
