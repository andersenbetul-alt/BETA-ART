Which tasks are worth automating, and which are not

Most automation projects fail on the choice of task, not on the technology. There is a simple test.

Beta Art · 2026-07-29 · 6 min read

The test

A task is worth automating when it is frequent, rule-based and low-judgement. All three. Miss one and you will spend more on the automation than the task ever cost you.

Frequent means several times a week, not several times a year. Rule-based means you can write down what happens without using the word “depends”. Low-judgement means being wrong is cheap and visible, not expensive and silent.

Good candidates

  • Retyping enquiry details from an email into a system.
  • Sending the same follow-up three days after a quote.
  • Renaming and filing attachments by a rule.
  • Producing the same monthly report from the same three sources.
  • Answering the twenty questions your inbox gets every week.

Bad candidates, however tempting

  • Pricing anything unusual. The exception is where the money is, and the machine has never seen it.
  • Anything a customer will read as a person’s voice. Automate the draft, not the send.
  • A task done twice a month. Twenty-four times a year does not repay three weeks of work.
  • A process nobody has written down. Automating an undocumented process encodes the confusion.

Start with the one you hate

Given two candidates of equal value, automate the one that annoys you most. Not because irritation is a good metric, but because the automation you resent doing the setup for is the one that never gets adopted. One working automation earns more trust — and more of the next budget — than a platform nobody logs into.

Keep a person in the loop where it matters

The most common mistake after choosing well is removing the human check to save two more minutes. A quote that goes out wrong costs more than every minute the automation saved. Draft automatically, approve manually, send automatically. The approval step is the product, not the friction.

Document it or lose it

The person who requested the automation will eventually leave, change role, or forget. If the workflow is not written down — what it does, what it assumes, what to do when it fails — it becomes a black box everyone is afraid to touch, and in eighteen months somebody quietly goes back to doing it by hand.
