What actually goes on an accession label

Every plate carries a record. Here is every field on it, what each one is for, and which ones you will be asked about years later.

By the archivist · 30 June 2026 · 7 min read

An accession label is not a caption. A caption is written for a reader; a label is written for whoever has to answer a question about this image in 2035, when the shoot is forgotten and the person who was there has moved on.

The label below is the actual record structure behind every plate in the archive. It is deliberately unexciting.

Identity

  • Accession number. BA-A1-001. Property, tier, sequence. It never changes and it is never reused, because it is printed on plates and encoded into every QR code that resolves to this frame.
  • Title. Descriptive rather than poetic. A buyer searching for a boatbuilder will not search for Hands, Remembering.
  • Category and keywords. Written for the buyer's vocabulary, not the photographer's.

Capture

  • Date and time, to the minute. Light is an argument and the clock is the evidence.
  • Place, named and, where it does not endanger anyone, co-ordinates.
  • Body, lens, exposure. Aperture, shutter, ISO. Partly provenance, partly so the shot can be repeated in the same conditions.
  • Frame orientation and aspect, so a designer knows before opening the file whether it fits the space.

Custody

  • RAW on record. Yes or no. There is no third answer, and no plate is marked verified without it.
  • Content credentials. Signed in camera, or not signed. Retro-signing is recorded as retro-signing.
  • Status. Draft, in review, verified. A plate can be published before it is verified; it cannot be sold as verified before it is.

People and permission

This is the section that decides whether a frame can be licensed at all, and it is the section most archives fill in last.

  • People in frame. Recognisable or not. If recognisable, named on the release rather than described.
  • Release on file, with a date and the four separate consents it covers: the archive, commercial licensing, promotion, and use of the person's name. The form this archive uses is published in full, in Norwegian, so anyone can read it before agreeing to anything.
  • Property, artwork and heritage permissions, where a building, a work of art or a protected site is the subject rather than the background.
  • Editorial only. A flag, not a footnote. It travels with the file into the licence.
The three fields you will be asked about years later: who is in it, who said yes, and where is the original.

The three that get asked about

Buyers rarely ask about the lens. In practice, three fields come back: the release, the RAW, and the status. They come back when a campaign is being audited, when an image is challenged, or when a legal team is reviewing assets before a rebrand — all of which happen long after the invoice cleared.

If the answer already exists in the record, the exchange takes four minutes. If it does not, it becomes a reconstruction, and a reconstruction is not provenance. That difference is the entire value of the label.

Fill it in at the shoot

The record is written the same day, not the same month. Every field you postpone gets filled from memory, and memory is the least reliable instrument in the bag. A record made before anyone doubted it is worth more than a perfect one assembled afterwards.
