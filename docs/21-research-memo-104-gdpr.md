## 1. Question presented

Where a photograph of an identifiable living person is accessioned into the
Beta Art archive under a signed model release, and is then offered on a
**worldwide, perpetual** commercial licence:

1. Does consent under **åndsverkloven § 104** (Norway) survive assignment to a
   downstream licensee, for the full protection period, without territorial
   limit?
2. Is that same consent a valid **GDPR lawful basis** for the ongoing
   processing of the subject's personal data (their likeness) — and if the
   basis is consent, what does the subject's **right to withdraw** do to a
   licence already sold?
3. Can the **Commercial** and **Extended** tiers be sold at all for plates
   showing people, given the indemnity Beta Art's own terms impose on the
   buyer?

These are three regimes that do not use the same words for the same thing, and
the archive's own policy says so in terms:

> "The interaction between `§ 104` consent, the GDPR lawful basis, and a
> worldwide perpetual licence — three regimes that do not use the same words
> for the same thing."
> — `docs/09-photograph-policy.md:170–172`

## 2. Short answer

**Not answerable on the sources available, and it should not be answered from
memory.** The controlling material is statutory text, EDPB/Datatilsynet
guidance and case law; none of it could be reached (§6). What *can* be stated
on verified sources:

- The archive's operating rule is already the conservative one. It does not
  rely on the § 104 exceptions for licensed work, it applies the strictest
  territorial rule to a worldwide licence, and it collects consent as four
  separate questions rather than one bundled checkbox
  (`docs/09-photograph-policy.md:88–100, 8–11, 154–156`).
- The specific risk the question is about is **already identified in the
  project's own record as unresolved and requiring a Norwegian lawyer**
  (`docs/09-photograph-policy.md:164–176`).

So the honest short answer is: *the house rule is defensible as a risk
posture, and its legal sufficiency is unverified.* Nothing in this memo
upgrades it.

## 3. Discussion, grouped by authority

### 3.1 Norwegian statute — åndsverkloven § 104

**Relied on (verified, internal):** the archive's own statement of the rule —

> "Any recognisable person | **Model release** | Norway: **åndsverkloven
> § 104** — a photograph depicting a person may not be reproduced or displayed
> publicly without consent."
> — `docs/09-photograph-policy.md:81`

> "the protection under `§ 104` runs for the person's **lifetime plus fifteen
> years** after the end of the year of death. An archive is a long-lived thing.
> A release should outlive the shoot."
> — `docs/09-photograph-policy.md:102–104`

**⚠ NOT VERIFIED.** The statutory text itself was not read from Lovdata. A
paraphrase returned by a general web search broadly matched the above, but a
search summary is not a source for a memo. **Required:** the current
consolidated text of § 104 from Lovdata, with the in-force date.

**Open on this authority:**
- Whether § 104 consent is *assignable* to a downstream licensee, or personal
  to the party who obtained it.
- Whether consent may be given for an unlimited term, or is revocable, and on
  what notice.
- Whether the lifetime-plus-fifteen-years period is a limit on the *right* or
  on the *remedy*.

### 3.2 EU / EEA — Regulation (EU) 2016/679 (GDPR)

**⚠ NOTHING VERIFIED IN THIS SECTION.** EUR-Lex is egress-blocked and no
article text was read. The provisions that must be checked, and the reason
each matters here:

| Provision | Why it is in issue |
|---|---|
| Art. 6(1) | Which lawful basis applies. If it is consent, 6(1)(a); if legitimate interests, 6(1)(f) and the balancing test. |
| Art. 7(3) | Withdrawal of consent — "as easy to withdraw as to give", and whether withdrawal reaches a licence already sold. |
| Art. 9 | Whether a photograph revealing e.g. ethnicity, religious dress or health is special-category data, and whether 9(2)(a) explicit consent is required. |
| Art. 17 | Erasure, and its interaction with an archive that has already delivered files. |
| Art. 44–49 | A **worldwide** licence moves personal data to third countries. Transfer basis unaddressed. |

**Open:** whether consent is even the right basis for an archive. A basis that
can be withdrawn is a poor foundation for a perpetual licence, and this
tension is the core of the question presented.

### 3.3 Norwegian implementation — personopplysningsloven

**Relied on (verified, internal):** the site states the applied standard —

> "Beta Art applies one standard globally: the strictest rule that could apply
> to a visitor is the rule we follow for everyone."
> — `legal.html`, *Privacy worldwide*

**⚠ NOT VERIFIED.** The Act itself was not read; Lovdata is blocked. Norway
applies GDPR through personopplysningsloven with national derogations, and
**§ 104 sits in copyright law while the likeness is also personal data** —
which of the two governs a conflict is precisely the unresolved point.

### 3.6 Third-country personality rights

**Relied on (verified, internal):** the archive already flags this as unsettled —

> "Germany's *Recht am eigenen Bild* and France's *droit à l'image* are the
> well-known ones; some countries require consent even to take the photograph.
> **This has not been checked country by country and should not be treated as
> settled here.** The safe operating rule until it is: a signed release
> covering worldwide commercial use, or no commercial tier."
> — `docs/09-photograph-policy.md:128–132`

### 3.7 The contractual layer — Beta Art's own terms

**Relied on (verified, internal):**

> "You confirm that material you supply … is yours to use, does not infringe
> anyone's rights and is lawful. You indemnify Beta Art against claims arising
> from material you supplied."
> — `legal.html`, *Responsibilities*

> "Where a recognisable person appears, a signed model release is held before
> the work is offered commercially; where a release is limited, the limitation
> is stated on the catalogue record."
> — `legal.html`, *People, places and property*

**The tension to put to counsel:** the indemnity runs from the *buyer* on
material the buyer supplies. On an archive plate, Beta Art is the supplier.
Whether the licence terms allocate the § 104 / GDPR risk to the right party is
a drafting question, and `docs/09` already names it:

> "Whether the Commercial and Extended tiers as drafted can be sold at all for
> plates showing people, given the indemnity in the licence terms."
> — `docs/09-photograph-policy.md:173–174`

## 4. Open issues

Carried verbatim from `docs/09-photograph-policy.md:169–176`, plus what this
memo adds:

1. Tier 3 in full, country by country, for the markets actually sold into.
2. The § 104 / GDPR / worldwide-perpetual-licence interaction — **the question
   presented here.**
3. Whether Commercial and Extended can be sold at all for plates showing
   people, given the indemnity.
4. The child-photograph position. Tier 1 takes the strict line by choice; a
   lawyer should say whether it is also the required one.

Added by this memo:

5. **Is consent the right lawful basis at all?** A withdrawable basis under a
   perpetual licence may be structurally wrong, whatever the release says.
6. **Third-country transfer basis** for a worldwide licence — not addressed
   anywhere in the current terms.
7. **Whether § 104 consent is assignable** to a downstream licensee.
