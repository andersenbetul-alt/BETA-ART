Seven pictures worth avoiding at launch

Seven frames that are still worth taking for yourself, and are not worth putting in a catalogue that has to earn its keep.

By the archivist · 21 April 2026 · 6 min read

None of the pictures below are bad. Several of them are the reason people pick up a camera in Norway at all. They are simply finished as a commercial proposition, and putting them in a new archive spends the one thing a new archive has, which is the buyer's attention.

The seven

  1. Aurora over wilderness. Enormous supply, and it is the single most convincing subject for a generator. A buyer cannot tell, and increasingly assumes.
  2. Fjord at sunset, no people. Every tourism library has hundreds. Yours will be the four hundredth good one.
  3. The perfectly composed cabin in snow. Sells a mood that is already fully served, at a price that has been falling for a decade.
  4. Reindeer at distance. Beautiful, generic, and frequently sold with unclear permissions attached to the land and the herd.
  5. The drone shot straight down. Striking once. It is now a default, and it says nothing specific about the place beneath it.
  6. The empty modern interior. Directly in competition with generated imagery, which is very good at rooms nobody is in.
  7. The staged handshake. Corporate buyers ask for it and then do not use it, because everyone can see it was arranged.

What they have in common

Each one is beautiful in a way that does not depend on anything having happened. Remove the photographer and the picture is still available — from a library, from a competitor, or from a prompt. There is no fact in the frame that had to be true.

If nothing in the picture had to happen, nothing in it is scarce.

What to photograph instead

The same places, with the thing that makes them specific left in. A named person doing work you can identify. An hour nobody drives out for. Weather that would normally send you home. A process halfway through, still messy.

These are harder, slower, and less immediately attractive on a screen. They are also the frames a buyer cannot obtain from anyone else, which is the only durable commercial position available.

Keep taking them anyway

The point is not that the seven are worthless. Take them, keep them, print them. The distinction that matters is between a personal archive and a catalogue: one is a record of what you saw, the other is a shop with limited shelf space.

Confusing the two is the most common mistake in a first collection, and it is not a mistake of taste. It is a mistake about what the shelf is for.
