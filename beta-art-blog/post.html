Essay · Provenance

The picture is not the proof. The paperwork is.

For a century a photograph argued for itself. That arrangement has quietly ended — and the cost of proving otherwise falls on the people still going outside with a camera.

By the archivist · 28 July 2026 · 9 min read

A photograph used to argue for itself. If you had the frame, you had the event: someone stood there, the light did that, the shutter opened for a fraction of a second. The picture was its own witness.

That is no longer true, and pretending otherwise helps nobody — least of all the photographer. An image indistinguishable from a photograph can now be produced by someone who never left the room. The picture has stopped being evidence of itself.

What replaces the picture's own authority

Records made before anyone asked for them. In this archive that means three things, and they exist whether or not a licence is ever issued:

  • The RAW original, archived within 24 hours with a checksum, under a filename that still makes sense in nine years.
  • The capture record — body, lens, exposure, time, coordinates — written at the shoot, not reconstructed at the edit.
  • A licence signed by the person who pressed the shutter, with a model release on file within seven days.

None of that is exciting. All of it is boring in the specific way that load-bearing things are boring. The value is precisely that the record predates the doubt.

Provenance produced after the question is asked is not provenance. It is an argument.

The three tests

Before a frame is catalogued it has to pass three tests, and it has to pass all of them:

  1. Provenance. Can we state the exact time, place, camera and subject?
  2. Not reproducible by a machine. Is this a specific person, a specific place, a specific light — something a generator cannot invent? Generic beauty fails here.
  3. A named buyer. Can we name at least one buyer who would pay kr 890 or more for this frame? If not, it does not belong in a licensing archive.

Plenty of good photographs fail the third test, and a few beautiful ones fail the second. They stay in the personal archive. That is not a loss; it is the point of having a standard.

Why this lands hardest on working photographers

A studio that generates imagery has no chain of custody to keep, and no cost in keeping one. The photographer walking back from the shoreline at 05:40 carries the whole burden: the drive out, the waiting, the storage, and now the filing discipline as well.

That asymmetry is worth naming plainly. Verification is a cost carried by the people doing the harder version of the work. The only sensible response is to make the record earn its keep commercially — which is exactly what a licence backed by a capture record does. Buyers who need to answer an audit question will pay for an answer that already exists.

The practical version

If you keep an archive of your own, three habits cover most of it. Write the capture record at the shoot. Turn on content credentials in the camera from day one — signing a file retroactively convinces nobody who matters. And number frames in the dullest scheme you can think of, because the dull scheme is the one that still works in a decade.

The photograph will keep doing what it always did: hold a moment of light. It simply cannot vouch for itself any more. Something has to, and paperwork is what we have.
