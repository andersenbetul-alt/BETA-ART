How long blue hour actually lasts at 59° north

This entry corrects something an earlier version of this journal said. The numbers below are computed rather than remembered, and they point the opposite way.

By the archivist · 9 May 2026 · 5 min read

An earlier note on this site said blue hour was shortest in the depth of winter this far north. That is wrong, and it is wrong in an instructive direction, so it is worth correcting in public rather than editing quietly.

Twilight length has almost nothing to do with how much daylight there is. It depends on the angle at which the sun crosses the horizon. In midwinter at high latitude the sun travels along a shallow diagonal, so it takes longer to sink through the twilight band, not less time. The short twilights are at the equinoxes, when the sun drops most steeply.

The table

Computed for 59° north, on the 15th of each month. Two columns, because photographers and almanacs mean different things by the phrase.

The May to July figures need reading with care. From roughly early May to early August at this latitude the sun never goes far enough below the horizon for real darkness, so the band widens and eventually stops being a discrete event at all. In June the numbers describe a long ambient dimming rather than a window you drive to.

What the numbers actually tell you

  • The shortest windows are March, September and October, at about forty minutes of civil twilight and roughly half an hour of usable blue. Those are the mornings to arrive early for.
  • Midwinter is generous, not stingy. Fifty minutes in January, and it arrives at a civilised hour.
  • Midsummer has no blue hour, in the sense of a window. It has a long low light instead, which is a different picture.
Thirty-one minutes in October. That is the whole margin, and half of it is spent finding the frame.

So: is the drive worth it?

Two rules come out of the table, and both are about arrival rather than technique. In the equinox months, be standing on the spot forty-five minutes before you think you need to be — the window is barely long enough to shoot in, let alone find a composition in. In the winter months you have roughly an extra ten minutes, which is enough to try a second position.

The other conclusion is less comfortable. If a frame needs blue hour specifically, it needs a scouting trip in daylight first. Thirty-one minutes is not enough to make decisions in, and decisions made in a hurry are what produce the technically fine, commercially useless frame.

How these were produced

Standard solar position for the date, solved for the hour angles at which the sun's altitude reaches each threshold, converted to minutes. No refraction correction beyond the usual allowance at the horizon, and no local terrain — a hill to the west removes minutes the almanac says you have. Treat the table as the ceiling, and check the sightline on site.
