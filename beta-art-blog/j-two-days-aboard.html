Two days aboard before the first frame

Two days of not photographing anything is not lost time. It is the only part of the process that cannot be bought back later.

By the archivist · 12 June 2026 · 5 min read

The plan was one morning out and back. It became three, and the frame that was eventually catalogued was taken on the third.

Nothing went wrong on day one. The light was good, the sea was workable, the pictures were competent. They were also, every one of them, pictures of a man being photographed.

What a camera does to a working deck

People do not become false in front of a lens. They become careful. Movements get tidier, hands find something to do, and the small unglamorous gestures that make a work photograph readable stop happening — because they are the ones nobody would choose to be seen doing.

You can see it in the frames afterwards. There is a particular emptiness to a picture where everyone is behaving well.

Day two: put the camera down

The second morning the camera stayed in the bag for four hours. Not as a technique — there was work to be in the way of, and being in the way is how you learn where to stand. Where the ropes run. Which minute the light comes over the breakwater. When it is safe to ask a question and when it is very much not.

By the afternoon the camera came out and drew no attention. That is the whole mechanism. Not trust in any warm sense — just the boredom that follows an unremarkable object being present for long enough.

You are not waiting for a moment. You are waiting to stop being an event.

Day three: the frame

05:12, hauling, no instruction given and none needed. The picture is not technically better than the day-one frames. It is a different category of picture: something happened, and a camera was present, rather than something was arranged so a camera could be present.

What it costs, honestly

Three days for one plate is expensive, and it is not affordable for every frame in a catalogue. It is affordable for the frames that carry the collection — and those are exactly the ones that cannot be arranged.

There is a commercial argument here too, and it is not sentimental. A generator can produce a fisherman at dawn on request, instantly, at no marginal cost. It cannot produce this fisherman, on this morning, with a release he signed and a record of the weather. The two days are what makes the frame unrepeatable, and unrepeatable is the only thing left worth selling.

If you are planning a shoot like this

  1. Ask permission for more days than you need, and say why. People agree far more often than photographers expect.
  2. Spend the first block without the camera, visibly. It is read as respect, and it is meant as respect.
  3. Get the release signed early, in person, while there is time to explain it properly — not by email a fortnight later.
  4. Write the capture record the same evening, aboard or ashore, before the days blur together.
