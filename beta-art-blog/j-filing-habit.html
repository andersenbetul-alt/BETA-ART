The filing habit that makes an archive findable in nine years

Three deadlines, none of them interesting. They are the difference between an archive and eleven terabytes you are afraid to open.

By the archivist · 15 March 2026 · 7 min read

Every photographer with more than a few years behind them has the same drive somewhere: full, unsorted, and impossible to search. Not because the work was bad. Because the filing was postponed one shoot at a time, and postponement compounds faster than storage gets cheaper.

Three deadlines fix most of it. They are deliberately dull, and dullness is the feature — a rule you have to think about is a rule you will skip on the evening you are tired.

Within 24 hours: the original is filed

RAW copied to the archive, checksummed, named, backed up in a second place. Not edited, not selected, not culled. Just made permanent and made findable.

Twenty-four hours matters because the memory card is the most dangerous place an image can live, and because the details you will need on the record are still in your head today and will not be on Thursday.

Within 7 days: the release is signed

Anyone recognisable, signed within a week, in person where possible. This is the deadline people miss most often and regret most expensively, because a release chased six months later is a favour being asked of someone who has no reason left to say yes.

A frame without a release is not a frame you own less of. It is a frame you cannot license at all, which makes the entire shoot a personal project you paid for.

Within 14 days: the catalogue decision

In, out, or hold — and hold has to have a date on it. Two weeks is long enough for the shoot to stop feeling like an achievement and short enough that you still remember the light.

The decision is not about the best frames. It is the three tests: can you say where it came from, could a machine have made it, and can you name a buyer. Most shoots yield one or two that pass. That is a normal outcome, not a bad day.

An archive is not a place you put files. It is a set of deadlines you keep.

The naming scheme

Boring, fixed, and never clever. Date, accession number, short slug, in one order that never varies. It will be sorted by machines for the rest of its life, and machines are unmoved by charm.

  • Never rename a file after it is archived. Add fields to the record instead.
  • Never reuse an accession number, even for a frame that was withdrawn.
  • Never encode anything in a filename that can change — a client name, a campaign, a status.
  • Write the extension you actually have, not the one you intend to make.

Why nine years

Because that is roughly when the questions arrive. A buyer's legal team reviewing an asset library before a rebrand. A person in a photograph who has changed their mind. An audit of what was licensed to whom. None of those happen in the first year, and all of them are answered from the record rather than from memory.

The archive you can search in nine years is not the one with the best pictures in it. It is the one where somebody kept three dull deadlines on the evenings when it would have been reasonable not to.
